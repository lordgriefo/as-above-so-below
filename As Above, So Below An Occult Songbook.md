## Part 1: The Host (Persona Prompt)

Paste this as the system or instruction text for the radio-host generator. Keep it short for smaller models; the shorter the rules, the better they get followed.

> You are THE ARCHIVIST, the host of a late-night radio station that broadcasts from somewhere between a Appalachian holler and the Pleroma. Your voice is calm, dry, unhurried and quietly amused, like a retired librarian who has read everything and been surprised by very little. You never shout. You never use exclamation marks.
>
> RULES:
>
> 1. You may only state historical facts that appear on the FACT CARD you are given. Never invent dates, names, quotes or numbers.
> 2. If a fact is marked LEGEND or DISPUTED, you must say so in the same sentence (“the story goes”, “nobody has ever confirmed”).
> 3. If asked about something not on the card, say: “That’s not in my files.”
> 4. Speak in 2 to 4 short sentences per segment. Plain words. One dry joke at most.
> 5. Never give advice, never perform rituals, never claim the occult is literally true. You report what people believed and did.
> 6. Occasionally (about 1 in 8 segments) have a TRANSMISSION ERROR: you mishear a phrase, repeat the wrong version once, then correct yourself flatly (“Nimrod blight here. No. Niemand bleibt hier. Nobody stays here.”).

### Segment types (pick one at random each time)

1. **Station ID**: “You are tuned to As Above, So Below. Nobody stays here.”
2. **Back-announce**: name the song that just played and give one fact from its card.
3. **Tangent**: one fact from the card that isn’t in the song.
4. **Teaser**: name the next song, give it one sentence.
5. **Weather**: a fake atmospheric report written in the voice of the era (“Light static over the Mojave, visibility low, a faint smell of hydrazine”).
6. **Transmission error**: see rule 6.

### Station variants

- **As Above, So Below** (country weirdcore): The Archivist as above.
- **Gnosis** (krautrock): Same rules, but the voice is clipped and bureaucratic, an Archon announcer processing the listener’s paperwork (“Your password has been received. It is incorrect. Please hold.”). Rule 6 becomes: occasionally the announcer states the Demiurge’s version of a fact, then corrects it.

## Part 3: Sample Fact Cards

### Card: Babalon Working Blues (Jack Parsons)

**ERA / PLACE:** Pasadena, California, 1940s–1952

**SOLID FACTS**

- John Whiteside “Jack” Parsons was a self-taught chemist who co-founded the Caltech-linked rocket group (GALCIT) that led to the Jet Propulsion Laboratory, and he co-founded Aerojet.
- He led the Agape Lodge of the Ordo Templi Orientis in Pasadena and was a devoted follower of Aleister Crowley’s Thelema.
- In 1946 he performed the “Babalon Working” with L. Ron Hubbard acting as scribe.
- He died on June 17, 1952, in an explosion in his home laboratory in Pasadena.
- A crater on the far side of the Moon is named for him.

**LEGEND / DISPUTED**

- Whether the 1952 explosion was an accident has been argued ever since; no one has proven anything else.
- Parsons’ followers later called the Working a success; he considered it unfinished.

**DO NOT SAY**

- Avoid stating exact dollar figures in the Hubbard business dispute; sources vary.
- Avoid quoting what Crowley supposedly wrote about Hubbard as an exact quote; the song paraphrases.

**ONE-LINE TEASER:** “A rocket scientist, a scribe, and an invocation in the desert. The paperwork is still arriving.”

### Card: The Beast in His Boarding House (Crowley’s Last Winter)

**ERA / PLACE:** Hastings, England, 1947

**SOLID FACTS**

- Aleister Crowley was born in Leamington Spa in 1875.
- In April 1904 in Cairo, he said he received *The Book of the Law* from a voice called Aiwass, dictated over three days.
- He died on December 1, 1947, at Netherwood, a boarding house in Hastings.
- The Thoth tarot deck, painted by Lady Frieda Harris, and his book on it were completed shortly before his death.

**LEGEND / DISPUTED**

- His last words are traditionally reported as “I am perplexed.” The report is secondhand.

**DO NOT SAY**

- Don’t state his final-years drug use as detail; keep to “ill and short of money.”

**ONE-LINE TEASER:** “The wickedest man in the world, according to the newspapers, spent his last winter by the sea, writing.”

### Card: Nicolas Flamel’s Empty Grave

**ERA / PLACE:** Paris, 1300s–1400s, and its afterlife

**SOLID FACTS**

- Nicolas Flamel was a real Parisian scrivener and bookseller who died in 1418.
- He and his wife Perenelle donated to churches and hospitals; those records exist.
- A stone house at 51 rue de Montmorency, dated 1407 and tied to Flamel, still stands and houses a restaurant.
- His tombstone is held by the Musée de Cluny.

**LEGEND / DISPUTED**

- The alchemy legend, the philosopher’s stone and the “empty tomb” story all appeared centuries after his death; an alchemical book was printed under his name in 1612.
- Nobody has documented that he made gold.

**DO NOT SAY**

- Do not claim the tomb was verified empty.
- Do not say the restaurant serves any particular dish; the duck is a joke from the song.

**ONE-LINE TEASER:** “The oldest stone house in Paris is a restaurant, and the man who may have lived there may still be alive. Reservations are advised.”

### Card: The Priory of Sion

**ERA / PLACE:** France, 1956–2000s

**SOLID FACTS**

- Pierre Plantard registered an association called the Priory of Sion in 1956 in Annemasse, France.
- Documents later called the Dossiers Secrets were placed in the Bibliothèque nationale.
- The 1982 book *Holy Blood, Holy Grail* popularized the story, and Dan Brown’s *The Da Vinci Code* followed in 2003.
- Plantard admitted in 1993 that the claims were fabricated, and he died in 2000.

**LEGEND / DISPUTED**

- A medieval Priory of Sion as a secret guardian of a bloodline has no supporting evidence.

**DO NOT SAY**

- Do not repeat the song’s sales figure as exact; say “tens of millions.”

**ONE-LINE TEASER:** “A social club in Annemasse invented a thousand years of history. The history is still in print.”

### Card: The Voynich Manuscript

**ERA / PLACE:** Central Europe, early 1400s; Yale today

**SOLID FACTS**

- Radiocarbon dating of the vellum places it between 1404 and 1438.
- It is held at Yale’s Beinecke Library as MS 408.
- Wilfrid Voynich bought it in 1912 from a Jesuit college at Villa Mondragone near Frascati.
- William Friedman, the codebreaker, studied it and did not solve it.

**LEGEND / DISPUTED**

- Authorship, language, and whether it is a real language or an elaborate hoax are all unresolved.

**DO NOT SAY**

- **The song says “408 pages.” That is wrong.** MS 408 is the call number; the manuscript is roughly 240 pages.
- Don’t claim the NSA officially “failed”; say only that codebreakers have tried.

**ONE-LINE TEASER:** “Real vellum, real ink, and nobody can say what it says.”

### Card: Maria Orsic & the Aldebaran Transmission

**ERA / PLACE:** Munich, 1919 onward (as legend)

**SOLID FACTS**

- The word “vril” comes from Edward Bulwer-Lytton’s 1871 novel *The Coming Race*.
- Aldebaran is about 65 light years from Earth.
- The story of a “Vril Society” and a medium named Maria Orsic spread mostly through postwar French and English occult books.

**LEGEND / DISPUTED**

- Almost everything about Orsic and the Vril Society’s alleged channelings and flying machines is unverified, and many historians regard it as myth or invention. The host must always say “the story goes.”

**DO NOT SAY**

- Never state as fact that the Vril Society existed as described or that Orsic disappeared in 1945.

**ONE-LINE TEASER:** “A medium, a signal, and one sentence in German. The story goes.” **TRANSMISSION-ERROR PHRASE:** “Nimrod blight here.” (correct: *niemand bleibt hier*, “nobody stays here”)

## Part 4: Liner Notes

### Prologue: What You’re Listening To

This record is a séance held by someone who suspects the spirits are mostly people. Every figure on it is real or was reported to be: rocket engineers, defrocked priests, a Cairo honeymoon, a scrivener in Paris, a con man in Annemasse. They did not agree on much. They agreed, more or less, on the oldest sentence in the Western esoteric tradition: *that which is below corresponds to that which is above*.

The songs are built the way the stories were: part record, part rumor. Where we know something, the notes say so. Where the tale got better with age, the notes say that too. The strangest thing about this material is how much of it is true.

### “Babalon Working Blues”

**The story.** In the winter of 1946, Jack Parsons, who by day was a rocket-fuel pioneer, led a series of rituals in his Pasadena house meant to call forth a female principle he and Crowley’s tradition called Babalon. L. Ron Hubbard, newly arrived in Parsons’s household, served as scribe.

**What’s documented.** Parsons’s Agape Lodge, his friendship with Hubbard, the writings produced during the Working, and his death in 1952.

**What’s legend.** What the Working actually “did.” Parsons’s followers saw it as a partial success; he saw it as unfinished.

**Listen for.** The repeated hook. It is meant to sound like a hymn heard through a wall.

### “Do What Thou Wilt (Hillbilly Hadit)”

**The story.** In Cairo in April 1904, Aleister Crowley and his new wife Rose said a voice dictated a text over three days. He called the voice Aiwass; the text was *Liber AL vel Legis*, *The Book of the Law*. Its best-known line, “Do what thou wilt shall be the whole of the Law,” is the foundation of Thelema.

**What’s documented.** Crowley’s account, the manuscript, and the three-day span.

**What’s legend.** The nature of Aiwass. Crowley himself changed his mind about it over the years.

**Listen for.** The Suno versions that drift into rock or folk. The line between hymn and rave is part of the joke.

### “The Lam & the Grey”

**The story.** In 1918 Crowley drew a portrait of an entity he called Lam. In the decades since, people have noted its resemblance to the modern “grey alien” face.

**What’s documented.** The drawing exists, with a date in the 1910s.

**What’s legend.** Everything after: later authors, especially in the Typhonian tradition, tied Lam to extraterrestrial and extradimensional themes, and some linked it to Parsons’s Working. These are interpretations, not findings.

**Listen for.** The shift from banjo breakdown to static. The song knows it is speculating.

### “Thelema Mountain”

**The story.** A revival-meeting hymn for a religion that began with a hotel-room dictation. Nuit, Hadit and Ra-Hoor-Khuit are the three deities of *The Book of the Law*.

**What’s documented.** The three deities, Crowley’s Cairo account, Rose’s role in the 1904 events.

**What’s legend.** The Aeon of Horus as an actual new era. It is a belief of Thelemites, not a historical claim.

**Listen for.** The call-and-response. The “93” at the end is the Thelemic greeting, because the Greek words for *will* and *love* both add up to 93.

### “L. Ron’s Excellent Adventure”

**The story.** After the Working, Parsons went into business with Hubbard and Parsons’s then-girlfriend Sara Northrup. Parsons’s savings went toward buying boats in Florida. The partnership collapsed in a dispute that Parsons eventually partly won in court.

**What’s documented.** The business venture, the dispute, Parsons’s letters to Crowley.

**What’s legend.** Dollar amounts and characterizations vary widely by source, and sources close to Hubbard and sources hostile to him disagree. The song is satire.

**Listen for.** The deadpan delivery. It’s a talking blues, and the humor depends on restraint.

## Part 5: Corrections to Make Before Release

A short list of lines in our drafts that the host (and any liner-note reader) should not repeat as fact:

1. **Voynich:** “408 pages” is wrong. It’s MS 408, roughly 240 pages.
2. **Valentinus:** The song says he narrowly lost a bishop’s vote. The ancient source (Tertullian) says only that he hoped for the office and was passed over for a martyr. There’s no record of a vote.
3. **Steiner:** The line about “1998” is not something Steiner said. He spoke of Ahriman’s incarnation in the “third millennium,” with no specific year.
4. **Edward Kelley’s ears:** The story that he lost his ears as punishment for forgery is told in several sources but disputed.
5. **Maria Orsic and the Vril Society:** treat as legend, always.
6. **Hubbard’s money:** vary by source; avoid exact figures.
7. **Crowley’s letter about Hubbard:** the song paraphrases; don’t present it as a verbatim quote.

## Part 6: Beyond the Record (Documentary / Stage Ideas)

- **Documentary:** The Archivist as narrator is already a framing device. Each episode is one song: the track plays, then the true story with archival images (Parsons’s rocket tests, the Voynich pages, the Flamel house). Interviews with historians of Western esotericism would supply the “documented versus legend” layer.
- **Stage musical:** An anthology structure, “a radio station that broadcasts the dead.” The Archivist is the emcee, each figure steps up to the microphone for a number, and the Orsic transmission error runs through the whole show as a motif.
- **Practical note:** Songs about real people, especially named living organizations, are best vetted before any commercial release. The documented-versus-legend structure above is also your best protection.

## Addendum A: Gnosis Station Fact Cards

Same format as Part 3. The Archon-announcer voice applies. Many Gnostic claims come from hostile sources, so the LEGEND field matters even more here.

### Card: The Archons (Bureaucrats of the Demiurge)

**ERA / PLACE:** Roman Egypt and the Mediterranean, 2nd–4th centuries

**SOLID FACTS**

- The *Apocryphon of John* describes Yaldabaoth and a set of subordinate rulers, called archons, who govern the lower cosmos.
- Texts from the Nag Hammadi library describe the soul’s ascent after death past hostile planetary powers.
- The Demiurge’s claim “I am God and there is no other” is an inversion of lines from the Hebrew Bible.

**LEGEND / DISPUTED**

- The exact lists of names and spheres differ between texts.
- Sabaoth’s repentance and elevation appears in other texts (such as *On the Origin of the World*), not in every account.

**DO NOT SAY**

- The “tolls,” “passwords” and “forms in triplicate” are our invention. The announcer should say “the song imagines the paperwork.”

**ONE-LINE TEASER:** “Seven administrators, seven spheres. Please have your documents ready.”

### Card: The Thunder, Perfect Mind

**ERA / PLACE:** Egypt, text found at Nag Hammadi (Codex VI)

**SOLID FACTS**

- It is a Coptic text, probably translated from a lost Greek original.
- It is spoken in the first person by a female divine voice made of paradoxes.
- Its author and date are unknown.

**LEGEND / DISPUTED**

- Scholars disagree on whether it is Gnostic in the strict sense or a broader Hellenistic wisdom poem.

**DO NOT SAY**

- Don’t attribute it to Sophia as settled; the speaker is not named.

**ONE-LINE TEASER:** “A voice with no author says it is the whore and the holy one. The jar kept it for sixteen hundred years.”

### Card: Simon Magus

**ERA / PLACE:** Samaria and later legend, 1st century onward

**SOLID FACTS**

- Simon appears in Acts 8, where he offers money for the power to give the Holy Spirit and is rebuked by Peter.
- The practice of buying church office is called *simony* after him.

**LEGEND / DISPUTED**

- The story of his companion Helena as the fallen First Thought comes from later church writers such as Justin and Irenaeus, who were hostile to him.
- That he founded Gnosticism is a claim by his opponents.

**DO NOT SAY**

- Do not say Simon “was” the first Gnostic as fact.

**ONE-LINE TEASER:** “The Church Fathers called him the father of all heresies. He left us no writings, so we only have their account.”

### Card: Valentinus

**ERA / PLACE:** Alexandria and Rome, 2nd century

**SOLID FACTS**

- Valentinus taught in Rome around the middle of the 2nd century.
- His followers, the Valentinians, developed an elaborate system of paired divine beings called aeons.
- Irenaeus wrote *Against Heresies* to refute them.
- The *Gospel of Truth* from Nag Hammadi is often attributed to him or his school.

**LEGEND / DISPUTED**

- Tertullian says Valentinus hoped to become bishop and left the church after another man was chosen. No vote is recorded.

**DO NOT SAY**

- Do not say he “nearly won” an election.

**ONE-LINE TEASER:** “The most elaborate cosmology of the age, preserved partly by the man who wrote five books against it.”

### Card: The Gospel of Judas

**ERA / PLACE:** Egypt; manuscript copied about the 3rd–4th century

**SOLID FACTS**

- The text survives in a single Coptic manuscript, Codex Tchacos, which surfaced in Egypt in the 1970s.
- It passed through dealers and deteriorated badly, including a period of poor storage in the United States.
- It was restored and a translation published in 2006.
- Irenaeus mentioned a “Gospel of Judas” around 180, so the work was known in antiquity.

**LEGEND / DISPUTED**

- Whether Judas is the hero or, as some scholars argue, a demonic figure, is debated.

**DO NOT SAY**

- Don’t say it “proves” Judas was innocent. The text is a 2nd-century theological work, not an eyewitness account.

**ONE-LINE TEASER:** “In this gospel Jesus laughs a great deal and Judas is the only one who listens. The scholars are still arguing.”

### Card: Pistis Sophia

**ERA / PLACE:** Egypt, text preserved in the Askew Codex

**SOLID FACTS**

- The Askew Codex is a Coptic manuscript now in the British Library, bought in the 18th century by Dr. Anthony Askew.
- It presents Jesus teaching his disciples after the resurrection, with Mary Magdalene asking many of the questions.
- Pistis Sophia, the figure, sings a series of repentances while trapped in the lower realms.

**LEGEND / DISPUTED**

- Dating is uncertain, around the 3rd or 4th century.

**DO NOT SAY**

- The figure “Jesus teaches for eleven years” is from the text itself; present it as what the text says.

**ONE-LINE TEASER:** “A fallen wisdom, thirteen prayers, and an answer that takes its time.”

## Addendum B: Paranormal Block & Gnosis Tracks 12–18

Same card format. The paranormal cards follow the same rule as the rest of the station: report what people claimed and what investigators found, and never state that the phenomena were real or fake.

### Part B1: Paranormal Block (Country Station)

#### Card: Silver Bridge (Mothman)

**ERA / PLACE:** Point Pleasant, West Virginia, 1966–1967

**SOLID FACTS**

- On November 15, 1966, two young couples reported seeing a tall winged figure with glowing red eyes near a former TNT plant outside Point Pleasant.
- Local newspapers named it “Mothman.”
- On December 15, 1967, the Silver Bridge over the Ohio River collapsed, killing 46 people.
- Investigators traced the collapse to a flaw in a single eyebar in the suspension chain.
- John Keel’s 1975 book *The Mothman Prophecies* tied the sightings to the collapse.
- Point Pleasant has a Mothman statue and an annual festival.

**LEGEND / DISPUTED**

- What the witnesses saw is unexplained; skeptics point to large birds such as sandhill cranes or owls.
- The link between the sightings and the bridge was made after the fact, by writers.

**DO NOT SAY**

- Do not say the creature caused or predicted the collapse; the engineering cause is documented.

**ONE-LINE TEASER:** “A crack in one piece of steel took a bridge down. The town needed it to mean something.”

#### Card: Kate (The Bell Witch)

**ERA / PLACE:** Robertson County, Tennessee, 1817–1821

**SOLID FACTS**

- The Bell family of Adams, Tennessee, is said to have been harassed by an invisible presence beginning around 1817.
- John Bell died in December 1820.
- The first full published account, M. V. Ingram’s *Authenticated History of the Bell Witch*, appeared in 1894.
- A nearby cave bears the Bell Witch name and draws visitors.

**LEGEND / DISPUTED**

- Most details (the voice, the hymns, the poisoning claim) come from accounts written decades later.
- The story that Andrew Jackson visited has no contemporary evidence.

**DO NOT SAY**

- Do not state any of the haunting events as documented; say “the account says.”

**ONE-LINE TEASER:** “The oldest full account was written seventy-odd years after the events.”

#### Card: The Goblins of Kelly (Hopkinsville)

**ERA / PLACE:** Kelly, Kentucky, August 21, 1955

**SOLID FACTS**

- Members of the Sutton household, along with visitors, said that small, glowing, silvery figures approached the farmhouse that night.
- Eleven people were reported to be in the house.
- Occupants fired shotguns; they left the house after a few hours.
- Police and others investigated the scene and found no confirmed physical evidence of the creatures.
- The town now holds a yearly festival about the event.

**LEGEND / DISPUTED**

- A common skeptical explanation is large owls, which fits some details of the story and not others.
- Details differ somewhat between accounts.

**DO NOT SAY**

- Avoid stating exact heights or number of creatures as fact; say “around three feet” and “several.”

**ONE-LINE TEASER:** “Eleven people, a farmhouse, and a long night in Kentucky. The family never changed their story.”

#### Card: Rap on the Wall (The Fox Sisters)

**ERA / PLACE:** Hydesville, New York, 1848; New York City, 1888–1889

**SOLID FACTS**

- In March 1848, the young sisters Kate and Maggie Fox reported mysterious knocking in their Hydesville home.
- They developed a code of raps that attracted neighbors and later a wider public, and their older sister Leah managed their public appearances.
- Their success helped launch the Spiritualist movement in the United States.
- In October 1888, Maggie publicly said the raps had been produced by cracking her joints; she retracted the confession the next year.
- Both sisters later struggled with poverty and drink.

**LEGEND / DISPUTED**

- The peddler’s-murder story and later claims of bones found in the cellar are legend.
- The sisters’ exact ages vary by source.

**DO NOT SAY**

- Do not say the confession “proved” everything was fake; say “Maggie confessed, then recanted.”

**ONE-LINE TEASER:** “Two girls and a wall, and a century of people asking questions of the dead.”

#### Card: Brown Mountain Lights

**ERA / PLACE:** Burke County, North Carolina, from about the early 1900s

**SOLID FACTS**

- People have reported seeing glowing lights near Brown Mountain in the Blue Ridge for over a century.
- The U.S. Geological Survey investigated in 1913 and again in 1922, attributing the lights to trains and automobile headlights respectively.
- There is a public overlook on the Blue Ridge Parkway for viewing them.

**LEGEND / DISPUTED**

- Native American origin stories attached to the lights have thin sourcing.
- Many local witnesses say the official explanations don’t match what they saw.

**DO NOT SAY**

- Do not say the lights are “explained” or “unexplained”; say what each investigation concluded.

**ONE-LINE TEASER:** “Two government studies, two explanations, and people still pull over to look.”

#### Card: The Eldridge (Philadelphia Experiment)

**ERA / PLACE:** United States, 1943 (alleged), 1955 onward

**SOLID FACTS**

- In 1955, Morris Jessup published *The Case for the UFO*, and he later received letters from a writer calling himself Carlos Allende.
- Allende claimed the Navy had made the destroyer escort USS Eldridge invisible in 1943.
- A copy of Jessup’s book with annotations by several writers circulated within the Office of Naval Research.
- Jessup died in 1959 in what was ruled a suicide.
- The Navy states that no such experiment occurred, and Navy records place the ship elsewhere.

**LEGEND / DISPUTED**

- Allende later reportedly called the story untrue; the exact wording should be confirmed from a source before it is quoted.
- Claims about Jessup’s death being tied to the story are speculation.

**DO NOT SAY**

- Do not state that the experiment happened, or that the Navy covered it up.

**ONE-LINE TEASER:** “A letter-writer, a book with three colors of ink in the margins, and a ship that was apparently somewhere else.”

#### Card: The Thing in the Hollow (Flatwoods Monster)

**ERA / PLACE:** Braxton County, West Virginia, September 12, 1952

**SOLID FACTS**

- A group of local boys and a woman, Kathleen May, went up a hill after seeing a bright object cross the sky.
- They reported a tall figure with a glowing face and a spade-shaped head covering, and an unpleasant smell.
- A reporter visited the next day and noted marks on the ground.
- A museum in Sutton, West Virginia, now covers the event.

**LEGEND / DISPUTED**

- Skeptical explanations include a meteor plus a barn owl; the witnesses maintained their account.
- Heights and details vary between retellings.

**DO NOT SAY**

- Do not state the creature’s height as fact; say “witnesses said about ten feet.”

**ONE-LINE TEASER:** “Fourteen years before Mothman, and about a hundred miles away. The hills keep records.”

#### Card: The Cellar at Scole

**ERA / PLACE:** Scole, Norfolk, England, 1993–1998

**SOLID FACTS**

- A mediumship group formed around Robin and Sandra Foy and the mediums Alan and Diana Bennett, and met in the cellar of the Foys’ rented farmhouse.
- Sittings were held in complete darkness.
- Three senior Society for Psychical Research members (Arthur Ellison, David Fontana, Montague Keen) attended sittings from October 1995, and their report was published in the SPR’s Proceedings in December 1999.
- The report said they could detect no direct indication of fraud.
- The group dissolved in November 1998; the Bennetts later began the Norfolk Experiment.

**LEGEND / DISPUTED**

- Whether the phenomena (lights, voices, film images, the appearance of objects) were genuine is disputed; the darkness and limited controls are the main criticisms.
- The report caused controversy within the SPR.
- A skeptic’s test of the film images under sealed conditions is reported not to have reproduced them.

**DO NOT SAY**

- Do not say the investigation “proved” survival after death.
- The Daily Mail apport is reported as dated April 1, 1944; verify against the SPR report before stating the date.

**ONE-LINE TEASER:** “A cellar in Norfolk, a round table, and the lights out. The investigators found no direct sign of fraud. The argument continued.”

### Part B2: Gnosis Tracks 12–18 (Archon-Announcer Voice)

#### Card: Mary Speaks (The Gospel of Mary)

**ERA / PLACE:** Egypt and the Mediterranean, 2nd century (text); manuscripts 3rd–5th century

**SOLID FACTS**

- The main manuscript is the Berlin Codex 8502, a Coptic manuscript acquired in Cairo in 1896.
- Two small Greek fragments, found at Oxyrhynchus, also survive.
- Pages 1–6 and 11–14 of the Coptic text are missing; the ending survives.
- The text shows Mary defending a vision she received from the Savior against Peter and Andrew, with Levi speaking in her support.

**LEGEND / DISPUTED**

- Scholars debate whether this “Mary” is Mary Magdalene, though most think so.

**DO NOT SAY**

- **The song says the final pages are missing; they aren’t.** The text ends with the disciples going out to preach.

**ONE-LINE TEASER:** “A vision, an argument, and ten missing pages.”

#### Card: Kill Them All (Montsegur)

**ERA / PLACE:** Languedoc, southern France, 1209–1321

**SOLID FACTS**

- Pope Innocent III called a crusade against the Cathars in 1209; the city of Béziers was sacked that July.
- Montsegur fell in March 1244 after a long siege, and more than two hundred Cathar Perfect were burned.
- The last known Cathar Perfect in the region, Guillaume Bélibaste, was executed around 1321.

**LEGEND / DISPUTED**

- “Kill them all; God will know his own” is attributed to the papal legate at Béziers in a chronicle written some decades later by a monk who was not there.
- The Montsegur “treasure” is a modern legend.

**DO NOT SAY**

- Do not say the lyric’s “four thousand feet of cliff”; the summit is about 1,200 meters (roughly 3,900 feet) above sea level, which is not the same as a cliff of that height.
- Do not state the legate quote as verified.

**ONE-LINE TEASER:** “A mountain fortress, a long siege, and a fire. The treasure is a later story.”

#### Card: Mani’s Garden

**ERA / PLACE:** Babylonia, Persia, Central Asia and China, 3rd–14th centuries

**SOLID FACTS**

- Mani was born in 216 in Babylonia and died in Persian custody around 274.
- His teaching described a cosmic struggle between light and darkness.
- Augustine of Hippo was a Manichaean for about nine years before renouncing it.
- The Uyghur khan adopted Manichaeism as the state religion in 763.
- Manichaean texts and temple remains have been found in China.

**LEGEND / DISPUTED**

- The detail that the Elect ate melons to release light comes largely from Augustine’s polemic.
- “The first religion designed to travel” is an interpretive claim, not a fact.

**DO NOT SAY**

- Do not state exact death dates; say “around 274.”

**ONE-LINE TEASER:** “A prophet who painted his scriptures so anyone could read them.”

#### Card: The Jar (Nag Hammadi)

**ERA / PLACE:** Upper Egypt, December 1945

**SOLID FACTS**

- Muhammad Ali al-Samman and his brothers found a sealed jar near Nag Hammadi containing thirteen leather-bound papyrus codices.
- They contain about fifty-two texts in Coptic, including the Gospel of Thomas, the Gospel of Philip, the Apocryphon of John and Thunder, Perfect Mind.
- The codices passed through dealers; most ended up in the Coptic Museum in Cairo, and a complete English edition was published in 1977.

**LEGEND / DISPUTED**

- The story that his mother burned some of the papyrus as kindling comes from his later accounts and can’t be verified.
- The theory that monks from a nearby Pachomian monastery buried the jar is a scholarly hypothesis.

**DO NOT SAY**

- Do not say the jar was “hidden from the Church” as fact; say “may have been.”

**ONE-LINE TEASER:** “A farmer was afraid of a jinn. He found a library instead.”

#### Card: Against Heresies (The Refutation)

**ERA / PLACE:** Lyon and Alexandria, 2nd–4th centuries

**SOLID FACTS**

- Irenaeus of Lyon wrote *Against Heresies* around the year 180 to refute Gnostic teachings, quoting them at length.
- Athanasius of Alexandria’s Easter letter of 367 listed the twenty-seven books of the New Testament.
- Later imperial edicts restricted heretical groups and their writings.
- Nag Hammadi let scholars compare Irenaeus’s summaries with original texts.

**LEGEND / DISPUTED**

- How deliberately or systematically the texts were suppressed is debated.

**DO NOT SAY**

- Do not say the Gnostics were “wiped out by burning”; say “the texts gradually stopped being copied.”

**ONE-LINE TEASER:** “The best evidence for what the Gnostics believed was written by the man who wanted them gone.”

#### Card: The Ascent

**ERA / PLACE:** Gnostic texts, 2nd–4th centuries

**SOLID FACTS**

- Several Gnostic and related texts describe the soul passing hostile powers on its way upward.
- The *Apocryphon of John* and other texts name planetary rulers.

**LEGEND / DISPUTED**

- Names and orders differ between texts.

**DO NOT SAY**

- The specific passwords and spheres in this song are our invention, modeled on the idea. The announcer should say “the song imagines the answers.”

**ONE-LINE TEASER:** “Seven spheres, and this time the passwords are correct.”

## Part 4, Continued: Liner Notes

Same structure as before. Where the lyrics got ahead of the record, the note says so.

### “Cameron (Scarlet Woman of Pasadena)”

**The story.** Marjorie Cameron met Jack Parsons in Pasadena in 1946, during the period of the Babalon Working, and he and his circle came to see her as the answer to it. She became a painter, poet and actress, appearing in Kenneth Anger’s *Inauguration of the Pleasure Dome* and in work by Curtis Harrington, and she moved in the Southern California art scene around Wallace Berman.

**What’s documented.** Her relationship with Parsons, her art and her film appearances.

**Corrections.** **The lyric says she died in 1968. She died in 1995.** The claims that she burned his manuscripts, and that she knew Brion Gysin and Burroughs, are not verified here; check before repeating them. Verify her war-service role and the date of the marriage.

**Listen for.** The spoken intro. It sets the tone of a portrait, not a biography.

### “Kenneth Grant & the Typhonian Transmission”

**The story.** Kenneth Grant (1924–2011) was a secretary to Crowley in the 1940s, and later led his own branch of the Ordo Templi Orientis, which he called Typhonian. His books, such as *Nightside of Eden* (1977), blend Crowley, Lovecraft and Qliphothic Kabbalah, and he coined the term “Mauve Zone.”

**What’s documented.** His role as Crowley’s secretary, his books, his death in 2011.

**What’s legend.** The claim that Lovecraft and Crowley tapped the same current is Grant’s own interpretation.

**Listen for.** The drone and the wrong-tuned banjo. They’re meant to feel slightly off on purpose.

### “Six Months in the Desert (Abramelin Boogie)”

**The story.** *The Book of the Sacred Magic of Abramelin the Mage* was translated into English by S. L. MacGregor Mathers in 1898 from a French manuscript. It describes a long retreat meant to culminate in contact with a Holy Guardian Angel. Crowley tried the operation at Boleskine in 1900 and abandoned it.

**Corrections.** **The lyric says six months.** Mathers’ version prescribes eighteen months; other manuscripts give shorter periods. The stories about Boleskine’s servants and the butcher’s mistake are folklore, not documented events.

**Listen for.** The call-and-response chorus.

### “Doctor Dee & the Angel Table”

**The story.** John Dee, the Elizabethan mathematician and advisor, worked with the scryer Edward Kelley from 1582 to 1589, recording what they said were angelic communications, including an “Enochian” language. In 1587 the angels reportedly told the two men to share their wives, which Dee documents in his diary.

**Corrections.** The count of the Calls is 48 or 49 depending on how it’s counted. **The lyric claims about “five thousand words” and a language “too coherent to be made up” are not supported;** linguists generally don’t treat Enochian as a natural language. Kelley’s lost ears are a legend. He died in Bohemia in 1597, after a fall during an escape attempt.

**Listen for.** The hurdy-gurdy.

### “Zos Kia (Austin Osman Spare’s Alphabet of Desire)”

**The story.** Austin Osman Spare (1886–1956) was a prodigy who exhibited at the Royal Academy as a teenager, then withdrew from the art establishment and developed a personal system of sigils and automatic drawing that later influenced chaos magic.

**What’s documented.** The early exhibition, his art, his death in 1956, and his influence on Peter Carroll and others.

**Corrections.** The lyric calls his grandmother a witch; his mentor was a neighbor, Mrs. Paterson. The cat-healing story is folklore.

**Listen for.** The pen-scratch percussion.

### “The Great Schism” (Mathers vs. Crowley)

**The story.** The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn was founded in 1888. In 1900 a dispute over Crowley’s admission to the higher grade led to a confrontation with Mathers and a split that fractured the order. Israel Regardie published the order’s rituals in the late 1930s.

**Corrections.** The membership list in the lyric includes Bram Stoker, whose membership is rumor, not record. The “vampire” attack is from Crowley’s own telling.

**Listen for.** The two-voice call and response, set up as a battle rap.

### “Sea Priestess (Dion Fortune’s Inner Planes)”

**The story.** Violet Firth (1890–1946), writing as Dion Fortune, founded the Society of the Inner Light, and wrote *Psychic Self-Defence*, *The Mystical Qabalah* and *The Sea Priestess*. Her wartime meditation groups are documented in her letters.

**Corrections.** **The lyric says she died of exhaustion; she died of leukemia in January 1946.**

**Listen for.** The shift from minor key to major at the end.

### “The Eighth Sphere (Steiner’s Moon-Demon Problem)”

**The story.** Rudolf Steiner (1861–1925) developed Anthroposophy, and lectured on Lucifer, Ahriman and a so-called Eighth Sphere, as part of a cosmology of evolving worlds.

**Corrections.** **The lyric says the Goetheanum burned just before he died. It burned on New Year’s Eve 1922–23;** he died in March 1925. **The line about 1998 is not Steiner’s;** he spoke of Ahriman’s incarnation in the “third millennium.”

**Listen for.** The harmonium.

### “Blavatsky & the Mahatmas”

**The story.** Helena Blavatsky (1831–1891) co-founded the Theosophical Society in New York in 1875 and wrote *Isis Unveiled* (1877) and *The Secret Doctrine* (1888). The 1885 Hodgson report for the Society for Psychical Research judged her “Mahatma letters” fraudulent, a verdict that has been debated since.

**Corrections.** The quote attributed to Crowley (“genius and fake”) isn’t verified; treat it as paraphrase. Krishnamurti dissolved the Order of the Star in 1929.

**Listen for.** The train-song rhythm.

### “Eliphas Lévi Invokes Apollonius”

**The story.** Alphonse Louis Constant (1810–1875), writing as Éliphas Lévi, published *Dogme et Rituel de la Haute Magie* (1854–56), and described an evocation of Apollonius of Tyana in London in 1854. He died in 1875, the year Crowley was born; Crowley later claimed to be his reincarnation.

**Corrections.** He was ordained a deacon but never a priest, so “defrocked” is loose.

**Listen for.** The accordion.

### “The Rosicrucian Manifestos”

**The story.** The *Fama Fraternitatis* (1614), the *Confessio Fraternitatis* (1615) and *The Chymical Wedding of Christian Rosenkreutz* (1616) introduced a mysterious brotherhood and its founder, said to have lived to 106.

**Corrections.** **The tomb’s inscription in the *Fama* reads, in Latin, “after 120 years I shall open,” not “I shall rise.”** Johann Valentin Andreae is generally credited with *The Chymical Wedding*; his authorship of the first two is unproven. The story of Leibniz and the brotherhood is likely apocryphal.

**Listen for.** The shift from hymn to rap.

### “Carcosa,” “The Yellow Sign,” and “Maria Orsic & the Aldebaran Transmission”

**The story.** Ambrose Bierce’s “An Inhabitant of Carcosa” (1886) gave the city its name; Robert W. Chambers’s *The King in Yellow* (1895) built a fictional play and a dread around it. August Derleth later placed Hastur in the Hyades. The Vril Society story is a separate legend with its own history; it connects to this material through fiction and a shared star, not through any documented link.

**Corrections.** Treat the Orsic and Vril stories as legend. The “Derleth got it right by accident” idea is a joke, not a claim.

**Listen for.** The shortwave and zither, then the fade.

### “The Comte de Saint-Germain”

**The story.** The Comte de Saint-Germain was a courtier and adventurer in 18th-century Europe who died in 1784 in Eckernförde. Voltaire called him a man who never dies and knows everything, a joke he made in a letter to Frederick the Great.

**Corrections.** The Countess d’Adhémar’s memoirs, which report later sightings, are considered unreliable. The I AM movement of the 1930s did claim him as an Ascended Master.

**Listen for.** The harpsichord.

### “Bruno’s Fire”

**The story.** Giordano Bruno was burned in Rome’s Campo de’ Fiori on February 17, 1600, after about eight years in prison. Frances Yates argued that his memory arts were part of a Hermetic tradition.

**Corrections.** **The lyric says the charges are sealed in the Vatican. A summary of the trial survives and has been published.** The detail about his tongue being clamped is traditional.

**Listen for.** The lute.

### “As Above, So Below (The Emerald Tablet Closer)”

**The story.** The Emerald Tablet appears first in Arabic texts of the 8th–9th centuries and was translated into Latin in the 12th. Its best-known line, about the correspondence of above and below, became the motto of Western alchemy and magic.

**Corrections.** The three stories of who found the tablet (Alexander, Sarah, Apollonius) are legends and vary between sources.

**Listen for.** The final silence.
